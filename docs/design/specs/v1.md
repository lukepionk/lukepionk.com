# Personal website v1

## Purpose

Help someone who has just met Luke understand what he builds, find useful
resources, inspect public work, and contact him within 30 seconds.

Exactly five sections: Header, Now, Resources, Work, Contact. No blog, long
biography, resume download, contact form, or invented portfolio projects.

## Content decisions

- Lead with building software; explain interests in everyday language.
- Mention broad work categories without employer names, internal product
  names, private links, performance figures, or organizational details.
- Offer eight resources in three groups, each with a concrete reason to open
  it. These are an initial proposed selection, not claims about Luke's personal
  reading history. Replace them with his own favorites as he supplies them.
- Start Work with this website. A substantial public project can be added
  when it exists. A polished personal page alone does not establish deep
  engineering ability.
- Publish the email explicitly supplied for public use and the confirmed
  LinkedIn and GitHub profile addresses.

Private career materials informed the draft but are not copied into this
repository or the website. The LinkedIn page was not retrievable during
research; its contents were not used to substantiate claims.

## Evidence and limits

The Stanford Web Credibility Project's guidelines recommend making claims
verifiable, showing relevant expertise, making contact easy, and presenting
information in a useful, appropriate design. The guidelines describe research
involving more than 4,500 participants, but date from 2002. They inform design
choices; they do not establish a conversion lift for a personal website today.

Source: [Stanford Guidelines for Web Credibility](https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines/index.html).

The resource shelf uses original publishers: AOSA, MIT, Google SRE, Anthropic,
Hamel Husain, Full Stack Deep Learning, Y Combinator, and Paul Graham. All eight
destinations were fetched successfully during the build. One broken proposed
YC link was replaced. Older material is retained for its ideas, with the 2023
bootcamp's tooling age called out.

Automated checks measure rendering and accessibility defects, not whether
people believe Luke is a capable engineer. That question needs human feedback.

## First measurement

Before making additional sections, ask five people outside Luke's current
team to view the page for 30 seconds, then hide it and ask:

1. What does Luke build?
2. What would you ask him about?
3. What evidence of his work did you find?
4. How would you contact him?

Record answers verbatim and whether each person finds the email and source
link. Initial acceptance target: at least four of five identify software or AI
engineering and locate a contact route. This is a small usability check, not
a representative study or a statistically powered experiment.

If people understand the role but want stronger evidence, prioritize one real
public project with a runnable example, decisions, and tests. More adjectives
will not solve that problem. If they misunderstand the role, revise the
headline first and repeat the test with new participants.

## Technical baseline

Static HTML and CSS; no runtime dependencies or external subresource requests.
Target under 100 KB for the initial page and assets, no horizontal overflow
at 320 px, usable keyboard navigation, reduced-motion support, and no automated
WCAG A/AA violations. Check screenshots on desktop and phone. Lighthouse is a
local lab measurement; report it separately from real visitor experience.

No visitor tracking in v1. Start with the short comprehension test; add
measurement only when there is a specific question it can answer.
